different doctors to pick from->• General Practitioners (GPs) / Family Physicians: Treat patients of all ages and manage everyday health needs, common sicknesses, and referrals.
• Internists (Internal Medicine): Focus exclusively on adult health, chronic diseases, and internal organ systems.
• Pediatricians: Specialize in the health and development of infants, children, and young adults.
• Geriatricians: Focus on the health needs and medical care of older adults.

Medical Specialists
Specialists complete advanced training to diagnose and treat targeted organs or complex diseases.
• Cardiologists: Experts on the heart and blood vessels, treating conditions like high blood pressure and heart failure.
• Dermatologists: Treat conditions related to the skin, hair, and nails.
• Endocrinologists: Manage hormone imbalances, metabolism, and conditions like diabetes.
• Gastroenterologists: Treat disorders of the digestive tract, stomach, and intestines.
• Neurologists: Treat disorders affecting the brain, spinal cord, and nervous system.
• Oncologists: Specialize in the diagnosis and medical treatment of cancer.
• Ophthalmologists: Medical doctors who diagnose and treat eye diseases and perform eye surgery.
• Otolaryngologists (ENT): Treat conditions of the ear, nose, throat, head, and neck.
• Pulmonologists: Specialize in lung and respiratory system conditions.
• Psychiatrists: Diagnose and treat mental health disorders using therapy and medication.

Surgical Specialists
These doctors perform operations to repair injuries, remove diseased tissue, or correct physical deformities.
• General Surgeons: Operate on a wide range of common abdominal and soft-tissue issues.
• Orthopedic Surgeons: Specialize in bones, joints, ligaments, tendons, and sports injuries.
• Neurosurgeons: Perform complex surgery on the brain and spine.
• Plastic Surgeons: Reconstruct or repair skin and body structures for trauma or cosmetic reasons

1. Routine & Preventive Care

Many people visit a doctor when they are perfectly healthy to prevent future illness and maintain a baseline of wellness.
• Annual Physicals: Routine health assessments, tracking BMI, and blood pressure monitoring.
• Vaccinations: Receiving routine immunizations, such as annual flu shots or travel vaccines.
• Screenings: Preventive laboratory tests for cholesterol levels, blood sugar (diabetes testing), and early cancer screenings.
• Lifestyle Counseling: Working with a provider to set fitness, dietary, or stress-management goals.

2. Acute Illnesses and Infections

Sudden symptoms or short-term infections are among the most frequent drivers of unexpected doctor visits.
• Respiratory Issues: Upper respiratory infections, severe colds, flu, sore throats, sinus infections, and bronchitis.
• Infections: Urinary tract infections (UTIs), ear infections, and sudden stomach bugs (gastroenteritis).
• Digestive Complaints: Acute bouts of acid reflux, severe changes in bowel movements, or abdominal pain.

3. Chronic Condition Management

Patients diagnosed with long-term medical issues require regular doctor visits to monitor their health, adjust prescriptions, and prevent complications.
• Cardiovascular & Metabolic Issues: Managing high blood pressure (hypertension), high cholesterol, and type 2 diabetes.
• Pain & Joint Disorders: Ongoing management for osteoarthritis, chronic back problems, and rheumatoid arthritis.
• Respiratory Conditions: Long-term management of asthma and Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD).

4. New or Concerning Symptoms

People often seek medical expertise when they experience "red flag" symptoms that require professional diagnosis to rule out serious health issues.
• Skin Changes: New or changing moles, persistent rashes, acne, or eczema.
• Unexplained Changes: Sudden weight loss or gain without a change in diet, or chronic fatigue/feeling tired all the time.
• Pain: Persistent or severe headaches, migraines, or chest pain.

5. Mental Health and Administrative Needs

Healthcare extends beyond physical ailments to include emotional well-being and logistical healthcare navigation.
• Mental Health Support: Seeking care plans, therapy referrals, or medication for anxiety, depression, chronic stress, or sleep disorders.
• Specialist Referrals: Visiting a primary care doctor to secure a referral to an out-of-network specialist, like a cardiologist or neurologist.
• Prescription Refills: Routine check-ins required to renew long-term maintenance medications.

1. Doctor’s Dashboard

Doctors require a clinical view focused entirely on patient health, immediate tasks, and medical history.
• What They Monitor:
	• Clinical queue: Upcoming appointments, patient check-in status, and reason for the visit.
	• Patient charts: Electronic Health Records (EHR), medical history, allergies, and active medications.
	• Lab & test results: Pending, reviewed, and critical diagnostic reports.
	• Task manager: Prescription renewal requests, referral approvals, and signing off on clinical notes.

Data Control & Limitations

• Retention (How long it is kept): Permanently or for legal minimums (usually 7–10 years after the last treatment, or until a minor turns 21), depending on local medical retention laws.
• What can be Edited: Clinical notes and treatment plans can be edited only until the session is locked or signed off. Post-signature changes require an addendum rather than a direct edit.
• What can be Deleted: Nothing. Doctors cannot delete medical records or history to maintain strict legal audit trails.
• What can be Archived: Past patient cases, inactive health conditions, and resolved allergies can be archived (hidden from immediate view but retrievable).
• Update Frequency: Real-time / Per session. The queue updates continuously as patients check in, while clinical notes are updated per session.

2. Administrative & Staff Dashboard (Reception & Nurses)

Administrative staff and nurses focus on clinic workflows, billing, scheduling, and preliminary patient triage.
• What They Monitor:
	• Scheduling calendar: Appointment bookings, cancellations, check-ins, and walk-ins.
	• Billing & Insurance: Invoice statuses, co-pays, insurance verification results, and outstanding balances.
	• Triage metrics (Nurses): Vital signs, weight, and initial complaints entered during pre-examination.
	• Communication portal: Patient messages, check-in forms, and incoming faxed documents.

Data Control & Limitations

• Retention (How long it is kept): Financial and scheduling logs are typically kept for 7 years for tax and audit compliance.
• What can be Edited: Demographic data (address, phone number), insurance details, and scheduling slots. Nurses can edit initial intake vitals during the check-in window.
• What can be Deleted: Duplicate or mistakenly created appointment slots can be deleted. Cancelled appointments are generally marked as "Cancelled" rather than deleted to track clinic metrics.
• What can be Archived: Expired insurance policies, old billing invoices, and past schedules.
• Update Frequency: Hourly / Continuously. Schedules and check-ins must refresh constantly to prevent bottlenecks.

3. Patient’s Dashboard (Patient Portal)

The patient view is a simplified, highly secure interface restricted strictly to their own personal information.
• What They Monitor:
	• Personal Health Summary: Active prescriptions, upcoming appointments, and immunizations.
	• Released results: Lab reports and doctor summaries that have been formally cleared for patient viewing.
	• Financials: Outstanding balances, past invoices, and online payment options.
	• Secure messaging: Direct messaging history with their care team.

Data Control & Limitations

• Retention (How long it is kept): Accessible for as long as the patient remains active with the clinic. If they leave, they can request a data export before the account is deactivated.
• What can be Edited: Personal profile details (email, phone, pharmacy preference) and upcoming appointment requests.
• What can be Deleted: Draft messages or unsubmitted intake forms. Patients cannot delete any medical history, bills, or official clinic communications.
• What can be Archived: Past messages or old notifications to clean up their personal inbox view.
• Update Frequency: Daily / On-demand. Data updates when a new action occurs (e.g., a lab result is released or a payment is processed).


Pharmarcy Dashboard (Pharmacy portal/dashboard)

1. Prescription & Order Management

• What is Monitored: Incoming e-prescriptions, pending refills, order verification status, and dispensing queues.
• Update & Check Frequency: Per session / Real-time. Staff must check this continuously to prevent dispensing delays.
• Data Retention & Compliance: Generally kept for 2 to 10 years depending on local laws (e.g., FDA, HIPAA, or state boards).
• Permissions:
	• Edit: Strictly limited. Pharmacists can append notes or clarify dosages after documenting verification with the prescriber, but they cannot alter the original digital prescription file.
	• Delete: Never deleted. If a prescription is canceled, its status is changed to "Voided" or "Canceled" to preserve the audit trail.
	• Archive: Automatically archived after the legal active period (e.g., 1 year after the last refill).

2. Patient Profiles & Clinical Safety

• What is Monitored: Patient demographics, known allergies, current medical conditions, and Drug Utilization Review (DUR) alerts (e.g., harmful drug interactions).
• Update & Check Frequency: Per session / Patient encounter. Must be reviewed every single time a script is filled.
• Data Retention & Compliance: Kept for minimum 10 years (or longer if the patient is a minor, usually retained until they reach adulthood plus a buffer of years).
• Permissions:
	• Edit: Demographic details (address, insurance) can be edited freely. Clinical notes or allergy profiles can be updated but generate a permanent log entry.
	• Delete: Patient profiles cannot be deleted.
	• Archive: Marked as "Inactive" or archived if the patient has not filled a prescription within 2–3 years.

3. Inventory & Supply Chain

• What is Monitored: Stock levels, reorder thresholds, expiring medications, and specialized tracking for controlled substances.
• Update & Check Frequency: Daily / Shift-ly. Automated systems track this in real-time, but manual inventory checks happen daily or weekly.
• Data Retention & Compliance: Transaction history (e.g., DSCSA tracking in the US) must be kept for 6 to 7 years.
• Permissions:
	• Edit: Quantities can be adjusted following physical audits or when corrections are made for damaged stock.
	• Delete: Inventory line items can only be deleted if they were entered in error and no transactions are tied to them.
	• Archive: Discontinued medications are archived so they no longer appear in active search results.

4. Telehealth & Clinic Communication Logs

• What is Monitored: Direct messages, video call logs, and clarification requests between the pharmacist and the prescribing doctor.
• Update & Check Frequency: Hourly. Messages must be checked frequently to resolve blocks on pending prescriptions.
• Data Retention & Compliance: Treated as part of the medical/pharmacy record and kept for 7 to 10 years.
• Permissions:
	• Edit/Delete: Sent messages and audit logs cannot be edited or deleted once finalized to maintain legal accountability.
	• Archive: Threads are automatically archived once an issue is marked as "Resolved."
